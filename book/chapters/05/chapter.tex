\chapter{Timers}
\label{c5:ch:timers}

{\large\itshape Counting time in hardware, and the arithmetic of not quite being able to.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Why a timer, and what is wrong with a loop that counts.
  \item The prescaler: five ratios, and the two places the ladder stops multiplying by eight.
  \item CTC mode, and why the period is \code{OCR1A + 1} ticks rather than \code{OCR1A}.
  \item Turning a frequency you want into a prescaler and a compare value, and the error left
    over.
  \item Frequencies no timer on this device can reach, in both directions.
  \item Extending a timer in software, so one interrupt can drive several rates.
  \item A blinker that keeps time while the processor is doing something else.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

The chapter starts with the counting loop, and its problem. Chapter~2 ended with an LED blinking at
about 127\,kHz because nothing was slowing it down. The fix is not a delay loop, and
\secref{c5:sec:timers} is about why. Then the hardware, once: a counter, a prescaler, a compare
register and a flag. Five paragraphs of datasheet, and then it is arithmetic for the rest of the
chapter.

Then the arithmetic: given a frequency, which prescaler and which compare value, and how far off
do you land. Work the table out rather than reading it; the rule is short and the boundary cases
are where it lives. Then what cannot be reached: an 8-bit timer at 16\,MHz cannot produce 1\,Hz.
Not badly: at all. And finally counting the interrupts, the software timer that fixes it, and that
lets one hardware timer drive four events at four rates.

Finish Chapter~4 first. The timer's compare match is an interrupt like any other and its handler
needs the same prologue, and its software timers are structures in SRAM, laid out and reached the
way Chapter~4's were.

Two predictions are worth making before anything runs:
\begin{itemize}
  \item what compare value gives exactly 1\,kHz, and whether ``exactly'' is really exact.
  \item whether two consecutive interrupts are the same number of cycles apart.
\end{itemize}
The second is this chapter's cross-check, and the answer is: sometimes.
